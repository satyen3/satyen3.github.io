I want to derive the star product from first principles. The principle is this: if I measure some property of the system, the system has that property.

The Cattaneo–Felder PSM interpretation of the star product, combined with the symplectic groupoid paper: the image of the $X$ field is a path starting at the point $x$. What the source and target of the groupoid is, is starting at $x$, and going one unit with the $df$ initial momentum to reach the source, and one unit with the $dg$ initial momentum with the opposite symplectic form to reach the target (which is equivalent to approaching $x$ with $dg$ momentum). I want to relate this to the fact that measuring $f$ means $f$ is determined, which means the phase space point takes off with $f$ as Hamiltonian (which is the same direction the source goes), and $f$ then $g$ means going as $f$ and coming as $g$.

I can derive it by saying $X_f$ is the direction where if the system moves $f$ remains constant. That's what it means for $f$ to be determined at that moment. But the thing is that the system actually moves along $X_H$, and $df/dt$ may not be constant. So I can't argue this using differential geometry.

I want $\pi$ at least implicitly as a derived quantity. I think the covector idea could work though. Measuring $f$ at $x$ simply gives me a number. I don't know if that's a constant or what kind of a function it is. It's literally an empty universal. So I think the covector $df$ and vector $X_f$ represent what we know and what we want to know, or the object and the subject. So $X_f(df)=0$ is negating the negation.

*From an older note (December 2025):*

I think the only way this makes sense is to not think of "points" as physical at all. One should not imagine a state as something fixed one can always see at all, because what we "see" are exactly observables, and those cannot be simultaneously observed. The fundamental ontology then is of states, and it exists in the sense that we can ask it questions about a certain aspect of it, one at a time. So a state in classical limit shouldn't be thought of as a point, but something that answers all questions consistently, meaning it doesn't change much on observation. So for example in the particle on a sphere, the phase space isn't a point, but a collection of states which answer consistently its position and momentum and all observables that depend on them. So we can label them using numbers $p$ and $x$. However in addition to this, we always know the limits of the system, so we know what states are possible. So take only one observable, the x-coordinate of the particle. We know that it HAS to lie on a circle.
