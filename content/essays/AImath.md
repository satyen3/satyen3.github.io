### The problem
As of late 2026, Large language models have arguably surpassed humans in several roles a mathematician performs during the course of their research. A machinic aggregation of "niche" areas of study, often supplanted at the scale of individuals to a handful of groups worldwide, is naturally expected to find unexpected connections and solve problems at the frontier level. This is not to say that I believe in the "convex hull" theory of LLMs being limited to building connections in the literature, but I want to take that as the starting point of their impact. 

Humans since the beginning of society have measured knowledge production in physical terms. An idea is only considered knowledge if enough people accept it, and if it shapes human labour in a tangible way. What complicates mathematics is that it is not just concerned with knowledge, but there is an aesthetic intent behind the people who pursue it. For some reason it tickles the part of our sensibility which lead us to find order and simplicity in the universe. The camps which mathematicians and AI math enthusiasts have divided themselves in seem to be along whether they consider one to be essential over the other.   

With this in mind, the big question is whether this new technology is here to create a fracture between these two facets of math, which so far have been surviving parallely in the academic institution. On one hand, the "purist" tech antagonists see any ideas coming out of the machine as irrelevant, purely on the basis of them not being given by a human. They consider mathematics to be a human endeavour, and letting machines construct arguments is seen as a violation of the very foundation of mathematics. They raise concerns about the vacuum of responsibility that the barrage of recent AI assisted/generated proofs have shown, and in some cases a complete lack of coordination with current experts in the field whose work these results derive upon. This by itself goes completely against how academic institutions deal with math, which I believe isn't enough of an argument to invalidate the results. The trick is to conflate the millennia old tradition of investigating mathematical ideas with the rules set up by institutions following the most efficient proliferation of consensus.

The topic of consensus however is nuanced by another property of machines: that they represent unlimited reproduction. If the goal is reshaping human labour, then being faster at printing unlimited permutations of true statements isn't relevant until the community has had time to internalize it. This concern is justified, but I believe it deals more with the policies we should adopt than the question of whether machines can actually create knowledge. What I find more convincing from the "purist" side is their eye towards the intentions of AI companies with math. It is naturally a great benchmark for an inventor to invent a machine capable of doing something nobody can understand. Sentiments drive value upward, which means higher returns for the venture capitalists.  

On the other hand the "accelerationists" believe a future is imminent where humans have been eradicated from the loop, and all new discoveries in math are now entirely machine made. Several proponents of this camp also view math as being reduced to its utility to society, with the "elitist pastime" aspect of it finally being severed. In imagining such a future we would need to make several assumptions. Is the machine able to come up with "interesting" questions by itself? Would there be enough short-term financial incentive to keep this project going? If the human math community is reduced to hobbyists, would it be allowed to interact with results produced by machines, and to what extent? With so much uncertainty it is hard to sympathise with this camp, and to imagine such a future where the last vestige of independent human thought has been captured by the flow of commerce is certainly a bleak thought.

### Acknowledgement

In a world where progress is measured in profit, where markets dictate whether a state can afford to leave a slice for the welfare of its population, funding universities which perform science is most likely an investment awaiting some form of return. For math, this meant an arrangement which let mathematicians freely explore widespread fields guided by their particular aesthetic visions as long as progress in the form of publication numbers and big named theorems kept being proved. The expectation is that people exist downstream who translate these new ideas into practical applications which drive concrete returns. A win-win for both camps.

In today's transitional period, a new way of investing in math has opened: as a benchmark for latest models to prove their analytical might.  This method not only promises short-term returns for direct investors, but also fulfills the criteria of "good math" by outdoing publication numbers and crushing big named theorems. We already see several of the best mathematicians leave the institution to chase what this new path offers. Taking their word for their intentions behind this switch, AI has already become integral to the future of math research, and it is only a matter of time until all frontier research has shifted from university halls to tech offices. In any case, we already see the fracture from before being carried out. There have even been reports of recruitment drives, of mathematicians working at AI labs visiting universities to talk about their latest AI derived results (although there have been questions of how much they understand the results they're merchandising). 

If it is the case that these machines keep getting better at fulfilling the objective metrics of "good math", it seems inevitable that despite objections from (currently a larger portion of) the mathematical community, the state is going to shift towards AI assisted research from the classic pen and paper style, by favouring grants which reward more AI use. This means gradually increasing reliance on AI tools and research carried out under employment of companies which own and invest in the models, and eventual corporatisation of the entire system. 

Another point left to be discussed is whether it is still worth shooting arrows in the dark: would AI assisted math be allowed to explore new theories and come up with new questions? I believe the answer is yes, since without it either the entire program comes to a halt, or they would have to rely on "real" mathematicians to digest AI written results and come up with interesting questions to feed the machine. 

### Method

It is a hot topic of debate whether LLMs can think, and downstream of that, whether they can come up with good conjectures. One of the cornerstones of math is that the journey towards solving a hard problem often necessitates new ideas and creative ways of arranging them to be formulated which end up being valuable on their own, whether it leads to solving the problem or not. It is pertinent to ask whether the new way of doing math is going to continue this tradition, or replace it with something else. Interpretation in models is still a new area of research, and it remains to be seen whether there is actual value to be extracted in the process of how a machine solves a problem. Failing that, can we come up with a new way of doing math in the presence of a black box which answers any question but with no explanation? 

An analogy which might seem useful is that of art. Art production since the 20th century has been largely reduced to mechanical reproduction, and artists being employed by companies which profit from its sale. When talking of "value" of art, it is implicitly assumed to be its value as a commodity in the market. The benchmark for arthouses is how profitable the art they aggregate is, and progress is measured in terms of how fast production can be carried out, including recent efforts in AI art production. However, this situation differs from math in two key ways: Art is relatable while math isn't, and the subjective nature of what society values in the art they consume versus the objective requirements for progress in math. There is more inertia in the peer-review system because it fulfills this objective requirement, and thus it might resist the same fate, or even converge to a different point altogether, which again remains to be seen. 

### Conclusion

As someone who is in the belly of this bloated beast, it is a challenging but also an exciting time. I am genuinely in awe of how fast LLMs have progressed, and the way the entire global economy has bet on its ubiquitous presence in our future. It is very tempting to see this as “the great equalizer” as many people call it, but as history has shown, this has rarely been the case with technological advancements. Soon enough the subsidized subscription plans will be repealed and access will again be stratified along purchasing power. The fracture is not between man and machine but between those who own the machine and those who used to do what it does. I will keep watching the story unfold from the sidelines of my math PhD UBI.
