It has been many days since I have cleaned my room. The objects and tokens which surround me have garnered an ever solidifying hold over my thoughts. A packet of instant noodles sits on my desk against the wall, its gashed carcass flickering in the tiny wind of the fan. Empty bottles of soda and energy drinks are balancing their bodies at odd angles, as if victims of an explosive massacre. Cigarette boxes, rolls of kitchen towel, ketchup sachets, takeout containers - these make up most of the foreign material populating my room.

Eliminating these reveals the content of their need - plastic jars of salt, pepper, sugar, a bottle of sunflower oil, tiny packets of butter, corn flour, mustard sauce, the odd half used clove of garlic - the precursors of my desire. With every calorie I digest they begin turning more opaque to my vision. I have always aspired to cook my own meals, but a paralyzing force compels me otherwise.

I want to write about what hunger really means for socialized beings like us. As the primary signifier for desire, it manages to permeate the barrier of the self with itself. When I get hungry, my inner world comes to a standstill. I get to hear an authoritative voice making its way to the barriers of my conscious experience. My aesthetic judgement, and along with it my will to live, is taken over by it.

It does seem to me that my place lies somewhere between the hunger artist of Kafka, and his bug. Within the body holding the uttermost disdain for agility and ambition, there lies trapped an aesthetic hunger. A hunger for food so special that it is conceivably unreachable. How my body relates to me is justifiably complicated. With food as their material plane of interaction, the feeling of hunger is the driving force for animosity.
With a bit more attention come into view the permanent residents - books, pens, pills - regulars of a hostel room. Things that supposedly make this room mine.

The three categories parallel my selfhood.

A flick of the chair leads me to the bed, which beneath the adornment of a pile of laundry stores all of my dreams in its crumples.

I do not belong here. The end date of my lease has already been printed and labelled. With every act of consumption, or rather disturbance - the flush of the toilet, opening of a pack of biscuits, squeezing of the tube of mustard - every act is a process of decay. Each recalls my final day here, while at the same time life itself. Each object here has been subject to my whims, and my mind is now forced to dance to the tune of their gaze.
